**The Front of the Card**

\[CLEAR, INSTRUCTIONAL\] The front of an insurance card contains the most critical information you'll need for registration and billing.

**\>\>\> HIGHLIGHT: Insurance company logo and "PPO" badge in top right \<\<\<**

First, look for the **insurance company name and logo**—here you can see it prominently at the top. Notice the **plan type** indicator in the corner—this one shows "PPO." Plan types like HMO, PPO, or EPO have different rules about referrals and network coverage. We'll cover plan types in more detail later in your training.

**\>\>\> HIGHLIGHT: "DREW MARTINEZ" \<\<\<**

Next, find the **member name**. This is the person covered under the plan. Here's something important to know: the name on the card might not always match your patient. **\>\>\> HIGHLIGHT: "Subscriber ID: SAB9873456789" \<\<\<**

\[EMPHASIZING\] Now, the **Subscriber ID**—sometimes called the Member ID or Policy Number. Look at this example: SAB9873456789. This is the unique identifier for that patient's coverage. It's typically a combination of letters and numbers. This number is essential. Without it, you cannot verify eligibility or submit claims. Double-check every character when entering it into your system.

**\>\>\> HIGHLIGHT: "Group Number: 15A855N" \<\<\<**

The **Group Number** identifies the specific benefits plan—usually tied to an employer or organization. In this example, it's 15A855N. Not every card has a group number. Individual plans purchased through the healthcare marketplace often don't include one. But if you see it, capture it.

**\>\>\> HIGHLIGHT: "Effective Date: 02/01/1985" \<\<\<**

Notice the **Effective Date**. This tells you when coverage began. When you verify eligibility, you'll want to confirm the patient's appointment date falls within their coverage period.

**\>\>\> HIGHLIGHT: Copay section \- "PCP Copay: $30", "ER Copay: $150", "Specialist Copay: $60" \<\<\<**

\[HELPFUL\] Many cards display **copay amounts** right on the front. Look at this section—PCP Copay is $30, Specialist Copay is $60, and ER Copay is $150. PCP stands for Primary Care Provider. These amounts tell you what the patient owes at the time of service for those visit types.

**\>\>\> HIGHLIGHT: "RxBIN: 601234" and "RxPCN: ABC123" \<\<\<**

You'll also see **pharmacy information** on some cards—the RxBIN and RxPCN numbers. These are used by pharmacies to process prescription claims.

**The Back of the Card**

\[REASSURING\] Now look at the back of the card. This contains important contact information.

**\>\>\> HIGHLIGHT: "For Members" section with phone number \<\<\<**

You'll find **customer service phone numbers**—notice there's a number for members to call about their coverage.

**\>\>\> HIGHLIGHT: "Providers: Submit claims to..." and "Customer Service: 1-800-555-1234" \<\<\<**

There's also information specifically for providers—including where to submit claims and a provider customer service line. When you need to call about eligibility or benefits, use the provider line.

**\>\>\> HIGHLIGHT: "Claims Filing Address" section \<\<\<**

Look for the **claims submission address**. This is where paper claims are mailed, though most claims today are submitted electronically.

**\>\>\> HIGHLIGHT: Disclaimer text at bottom \<\<\<**

## **LESSON 2: Real-Time Eligibility Verification**

**Duration:** 4-5 minutes (approximately 580 words) **Lesson Type:** Process/Workflow

---

### **VIDEO SCRIPT**

\[WARM, ENGAGING\] Now that you know how to read an insurance card, let's talk about what you do with that information. You verify eligibility. This is one of the most important tasks you'll perform—and when done correctly, it prevents problems down the road for everyone.

\[CLEAR, FOUNDATIONAL\] **What Is Eligibility Verification?**

Eligibility verification confirms that a patient's insurance coverage is active and will pay for services at your clinic. Just because someone has an insurance card doesn't mean their coverage is current. People change jobs, policies lapse, or coverage details change. Verification catches these issues before they become billing problems.

\[REASSURING\] Most clinics today use **real-time eligibility**—meaning you can check a patient's coverage electronically in seconds. Your practice management system or EHR likely has a button or link that says something like "Check Eligibility" or "Verify Insurance." When you click it, the system sends the patient's information to the insurance company and returns results almost immediately.

\[INSTRUCTIONAL\] **When Should You Verify?**

Here's a simple guide:

For **new patients**, always verify at the time of registration—ideally 48 to 72 hours before their appointment. This gives you time to contact the patient if there's an issue.

For **existing patients with new insurance**, verify as soon as you learn about the change.

For **established patients with the same insurance**, verify monthly—or at minimum, before any visit where more than 30 days have passed since the last check.

For **procedures or high-dollar services**, verify 48 to 72 hours in advance. These visits often have specific authorization requirements you'll want to catch early.

\[HELPFUL, GUIDING\] **What to Look For in the Results**

When eligibility comes back, you're checking several things:

First—**Is coverage active?** Look for the effective date and make sure it covers the appointment date. If coverage ended last month, the patient may not realize it yet.

Second—**Is your clinic in-network?** The results often indicate network status. If you're out of network, the patient may face higher costs or no coverage at all.

Third—**What are the patient's cost-sharing amounts?** This is where you'll see the copay, deductible status, and coinsurance. We'll cover each of these in upcoming lessons, but for now, know that this information tells you what the patient will owe.

Fourth—**Are there any special requirements?** Some plans require referrals or prior authorizations for certain services. The eligibility response may flag these.

\[EMPHASIZING\] **What If Verification Fails?**

Sometimes eligibility doesn't return a clear answer. Maybe the Member ID was entered incorrectly, or the system timed out. Here's what to do:

Double-check that you entered the Member ID and date of birth exactly as shown on the card. Even one wrong digit can cause a failure.

If it still doesn't work, try again closer to the appointment date—sometimes there's a delay in the insurance company's system.

As a last resort, call the insurance company's provider line. Have the patient's information ready, and document everything—the date, time, who you spoke with, and what they confirmed.

\[WARM, CONFIDENT CLOSE\] **Why This Matters**

Verifying eligibility protects everyone. It protects the clinic from claim denials. It protects patients from surprise bills. And it protects you from difficult conversations that could have been avoided.

Make verification part of your routine, and you'll catch problems before they become crises.

Great work—you're building a strong foundation.

## **LESSON 3: Understanding Copays**

**Duration:** 3-4 minutes (approximately 480 words) **Lesson Type:** Concept Explanation

---

### **VIDEO SCRIPT**

\[WARM, FRIENDLY\] When a patient checks in, one of the first things you'll do is collect their copay. But what exactly is a copay, and why does it matter? Let's make sure you understand this clearly—because you'll be explaining it to patients, too.

\[CLEAR, INSTRUCTIONAL\] **What Is a Copay?**

A copay—short for copayment—is a fixed dollar amount that a patient pays at the time of service. It's their share of the cost for that visit.

Here's what makes copays straightforward: the amount is the same every time for that type of service. If the copay for a primary care visit is $25, the patient pays $25—whether the visit takes 10 minutes or 45 minutes, whether it's for a cold or a complex concern.

\[HELPFUL\] **Where to Find the Copay Amount**

You can find copay information in two places:

First, check the **insurance card itself**. Many cards list copay amounts right on the front. You might see "Office Visit: $30" or "PCP: $25 / Specialist: $50."

Second, the **eligibility verification response** will show copay amounts. This is the most reliable source because it reflects the patient's current benefits.

\[EMPHASIZING\] **Different Copays for Different Services**

Not all copays are the same. Patients typically have different copay amounts depending on where they receive care:

A **primary care visit** usually has the lowest copay—often $20 to $40.

A **specialist visit** typically costs more—perhaps $40 to $75.

**Urgent care** visits often have a moderate copay, higher than primary care but lower than the emergency room.

**Emergency room** copays are usually the highest—sometimes $150 or more.

The insurance company structures these amounts intentionally—to encourage patients to seek care at the most appropriate level.

\[REASSURING\] **When to Collect the Copay**

Best practice is to collect copays at check-in, before the patient sees the provider. This is the expectation of most insurance contracts, and it's easier to collect payment when the patient is right there at your desk.

Simply state the amount clearly: "Your copay today is $30. Will that be card, cash, or check?"

Be matter-of-fact and friendly. This is a normal part of every visit.

\[HELPFUL, PRACTICAL\] **Handling Copay Questions**

Sometimes patients are surprised by their copay amount—especially if it recently changed. If a patient questions the amount, you can:

Show them where it appears on their insurance card, or explain that the amount came directly from their insurance company's eligibility response.

If they're still uncertain, offer to provide a receipt and suggest they contact their insurance company's member services line to confirm their benefits.

\[WARM CLOSE\] **Your Role**

You're not just collecting money—you're helping patients understand their financial responsibility upfront. That transparency builds trust and prevents confusion later.

Copays are one piece of the cost-sharing puzzle. Next, we'll look at deductibles and out-of-pocket maximums—concepts that go hand in hand with what you've just learned.

You're doing great.

## **LESSON 4: Deductibles and Out-of-Pocket Maximum**

**Duration:** 4 minutes (approximately 530 words) **Lesson Type:** Concept Explanation

---

### **VIDEO SCRIPT**

\[WARM, ENGAGING\] Understanding deductibles is one of the trickiest parts of health insurance—for patients and staff alike. But once this concept clicks, you'll be able to answer patient questions with confidence. Let's break it down.

\[CLEAR, FOUNDATIONAL\] **What Is a Deductible?**

A deductible is the amount a patient must pay out of their own pocket before their insurance starts covering a larger share of costs.

Think of it like a threshold. Until the patient crosses that threshold by paying the deductible amount, they're responsible for more of the bill.

For example, if a patient has a $1,500 deductible, they pay the first $1,500 of covered medical expenses each year. After that, their insurance begins paying a larger portion.

\[HELPFUL\] **How to See Deductible Status**

When you run eligibility verification, the response will show you how much of the patient's deductible has been met.

You might see something like: "Deductible: $1,500 / Met: $800 / Remaining: $700."

This tells you the patient still has $700 to pay before their deductible is satisfied. Any services they receive will first apply toward that remaining $700.

\[REASSURING\] **Why This Matters at the Front Desk**

When a patient hasn't met their deductible, they may owe more than just a copay. For certain services—like lab work, imaging, or procedures—the patient could be responsible for the full cost until their deductible is met.

This doesn't mean you need to calculate exact amounts at check-in. But knowing the deductible status helps you set appropriate expectations: "I see you still have some deductible remaining, so you may receive a bill after your insurance processes the claim."

\[CLEAR, INSTRUCTIONAL\] **Individual vs. Family Deductibles**

Many plans have both an individual deductible and a family deductible.

The **individual deductible** applies to each person separately. Once one family member meets their individual deductible, their cost-sharing changes—even if other family members haven't met theirs yet.

The **family deductible** is a combined total for all covered family members. Once the family deductible is met, cost-sharing changes for everyone on the plan.

This can get complicated, so when patients have questions about family deductibles, it's appropriate to refer them to their insurance company's member services.

\[TRANSITIONING\] **What Is the Out-of-Pocket Maximum?**

Here's the good news for patients: there's a limit to how much they'll pay in a year. It's called the **out-of-pocket maximum**.

Once a patient reaches their out-of-pocket maximum, their insurance pays 100% of covered services for the rest of the plan year. They're done paying—no more copays, no more coinsurance, no more deductible.

The out-of-pocket maximum includes deductible payments, copays, and coinsurance. It does not include monthly premiums.

\[PRACTICAL APPLICATION\] **What This Means for You**

You won't calculate these amounts manually. Your eligibility verification will show deductible and out-of-pocket maximum status. Your job is to:

Recognize when a patient has deductible remaining—and set expectations accordingly.

Understand that patients who've met their out-of-pocket maximum may owe nothing beyond their visit.

Be ready to explain these concepts in simple terms when patients have questions.

\[WARM CLOSE\] This is valuable knowledge. Patients often don't understand their own benefits, and your ability to explain deductibles clearly makes their experience better.

Next up: coinsurance—another piece of the cost-sharing picture.

## **LESSON 5: Coinsurance Calculations**

**Duration:** 3-4 minutes (approximately 450 words) **Lesson Type:** Concept Explanation

---

### **VIDEO SCRIPT**

\[WARM, ENCOURAGING\] You've learned about copays—fixed amounts patients pay. Now let's talk about coinsurance—which works a little differently. Instead of a fixed dollar amount, coinsurance is a percentage of the cost.

\[CLEAR, INSTRUCTIONAL\] **What Is Coinsurance?**

Coinsurance is the percentage of a covered medical bill that the patient is responsible for after they've met their deductible.

You'll often see coinsurance expressed as a ratio, like "80/20." This means the insurance company pays 80% of the covered cost, and the patient pays 20%.

\[HELPFUL, PRACTICAL\] **When Does Coinsurance Apply?**

Coinsurance typically kicks in after the patient has met their annual deductible. Before the deductible is met, the patient may be responsible for the entire allowed amount. After the deductible is met, the insurance company and patient share costs according to the coinsurance percentage.

Let's walk through an example:

A patient has a procedure that costs $1,000. Their deductible has already been met for the year. Their plan has 80/20 coinsurance.

The insurance company pays 80%—that's $800. The patient pays 20%—that's $200.

That $200 is the patient's coinsurance responsibility.

\[REASSURING\] **You Won't Calculate This at Check-In**

Here's the important thing to understand: you won't be doing these calculations yourself at the front desk. The billing department and the insurance company work out the exact amounts after the claim is processed.

Your role is to understand the concept so you can explain it to patients when they ask: "Why do I owe money after my insurance paid?"

The answer is coinsurance—their share of the cost based on their plan's percentage.

\[CLEAR, EMPHASIZING\] **Finding Coinsurance Information**

Coinsurance rates appear in the eligibility verification response. You might see: "In-Network Coinsurance: 20%" or "Member Responsibility: 20%."

Some plans have different coinsurance rates for in-network versus out-of-network providers. Out-of-network coinsurance is usually much higher—sometimes 40% or 50%. This is another reason why staying in-network matters for patients.

\[HELPFUL\] **Coinsurance and the Out-of-Pocket Maximum**

Here's good news for patients: coinsurance payments count toward their out-of-pocket maximum. Once they reach that maximum, their insurance covers 100%—no more coinsurance for the rest of the plan year.

\[WARM CLOSE\] **Bringing It Together**

Let's recap the cost-sharing picture:

Copays are fixed amounts paid at the time of service. Deductibles are amounts patients pay before insurance pays more. Coinsurance is the percentage patients pay after meeting their deductible. Out-of-pocket maximum is the most a patient pays in a year.

Understanding how these work together helps you guide patients through their financial responsibilities with confidence.

You're building real expertise here. Let's move to our final lesson—collecting patient payments.

## **LESSON 6: Collecting Patient Payments**

**Duration:** 4 minutes (approximately 520 words) **Lesson Type:** Process/Best Practices

---

### **VIDEO SCRIPT**

\[WARM, PROFESSIONAL\] Everything we've covered—insurance cards, eligibility, copays, deductibles, coinsurance—comes together at one critical moment: collecting payment from the patient. This can feel awkward at first, but with the right approach, it becomes a natural part of every check-in.

\[ENCOURAGING\] Let's talk about how to do this professionally and confidently.

\[CLEAR, INSTRUCTIONAL\] **Collect at Check-In**

The best time to collect patient payments is at check-in—before the patient sees the provider. Here's why:

The patient is right there at your desk, ready to complete the check-in process. Payment is simply one step in that process.

Most insurance contracts require copay collection at the time of service. It's not optional—it's expected.

Collecting upfront reduces outstanding balances and awkward follow-up calls later.

\[PRACTICAL, HELPFUL\] **How to Ask for Payment**

Keep it simple and direct. After you've verified the patient's information, say:

"Your copay today is $35. Will that be card, cash, or check?"

Notice what this does: it states the amount clearly, assumes payment will happen, and offers options. You're not asking if they want to pay—you're asking how.

Be friendly and matter-of-fact. This is routine, and your tone should reflect that.

\[REASSURING\] **Handling Outstanding Balances**

Sometimes patients have a balance from a previous visit—maybe from coinsurance or a deductible that applied after their insurance processed the claim.

At check-in, you might say:

"I also see you have a balance of $47 from your last visit. Would you like to take care of that today as well?"

Offer, but don't demand. If the patient can't pay the full balance, partial payment is better than none. You can also mention payment plan options if your clinic offers them.

The key is to address balances—not ignore them. Every mention is an opportunity to collect.

\[CLEAR, PRACTICAL\] **Handling Discrepancies**

Sometimes patients question the copay amount. Maybe their card shows $25 but eligibility says $40. Plan benefits do change—often at the start of a new year.

Stay calm and explain:

"The amount I'm seeing came directly from your insurance company's current records. If you'd like to confirm, you can call the member services number on your card."

Offer a receipt, and let them follow up with their insurer. You're not the one setting the copay—you're simply collecting what's indicated.

\[EMPHASIZING\] **Always Provide a Receipt**

Every payment should generate a receipt. Hand it to the patient or offer to email it. This protects both the patient and your clinic, and it's simply good practice.

\[WARM, CONFIDENT CLOSE\] **Your Mindset Matters**

Collecting payment isn't asking for a favor. It's a normal, expected part of healthcare. Patients understand they have financial responsibilities—your job is to communicate those responsibilities clearly and professionally.

When you approach payment collection with confidence, patients respond in kind. You're helping them take care of their obligation in a straightforward way.

And that's it—you've completed the insurance essentials. You now understand how to read an insurance card, verify eligibility, and explain the key components of patient cost-sharing. These skills will serve you every single day.

Well done. You're ready for the next step in your training.
