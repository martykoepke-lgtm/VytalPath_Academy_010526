# **LESSON 1: Introduction to Health Insurance**

**Duration:** 3 minutes  
 **Learning Objective:** Understand why health insurance exists and how it functions as a system connecting patients, providers, and payers.

---

## **SCRIPT**

### **\[0:00-0:25\] OPENING \- The Problem Insurance Solves**

**VISUAL:** Split screen—left side shows hospital bill ($47,000), right side shows worried patient

**NARRATOR:** "Imagine you break your leg and need surgery. The hospital bill? Forty-seven thousand dollars. Most people don't have that kind of money sitting in their bank account.

This is the problem health insurance solves. It spreads the cost of expensive medical care across large groups of people, so when something happens to YOU, you're not facing that bill alone.

Let's look at how this actually works."

---

### **\[0:25-1:15\] HOW INSURANCE WORKS \- The Three Players**

**VISUAL:** Triangle diagram with three icons—Patient (person icon), Provider (stethoscope/clinic icon), Payer (building/insurance icon). Arrows connect all three.

**NARRATOR:** "Health insurance involves three players, and understanding their relationship is key to your front office role.

\[Patient icon highlights\] First, the PATIENT. They pay a monthly fee—called a premium—to an insurance company. In exchange, the insurance company promises to help pay for their medical care.

\[Payer icon highlights\] Second, the PAYER—that's the insurance company. They collect premiums from thousands of patients. When someone needs care, the insurance company pays part of the bill. They're the middleman between patients and healthcare providers.

\[Provider icon highlights\] Third, the PROVIDER—that's us. The clinic, the hospital, the doctor's office. We provide the care and then bill the insurance company for our services.

\[Arrows animate between all three\] Here's where it gets interesting: The patient pays premiums to the payer. The provider bills the payer for services. And the patient pays their share—copays, deductibles, coinsurance—to the provider.

Your job at the front desk? You're right in the middle of all three relationships. You collect information from patients, verify coverage with payers, and collect what the patient owes."

---

### **\[1:15-2:00\] WHY THIS MATTERS \- Risk Pooling**

**VISUAL:** Animation showing 1,000 people icons, then showing 50 of them highlighted as "needed major care this year"

**NARRATOR:** "You might wonder—how can insurance companies afford to pay these huge medical bills?

It's called risk pooling. Here's how it works.

\[Animation: 1,000 people appear\] Let's say an insurance company has one thousand members, each paying five hundred dollars a month in premiums. That's five million dollars coming in every month.

\[Animation: 50 people highlight in different color\] In any given month, maybe fifty of those members need significant medical care. The insurance company uses the premiums from ALL one thousand members to pay for the care of those fifty.

\[Animation: Shows money flowing from the group to the 50\] Most months, most people don't need expensive care. But when they DO, the money is there—because everyone has been contributing to the pool.

This is why insurance works. The healthy subsidize the sick. And someday, when YOU need expensive care, the pool will be there for you."

---

### **\[2:00-2:40\] THE FRONT OFFICE CONNECTION**

**VISUAL:** Front desk scene with three thought bubbles: "Is this patient covered?", "What do they owe?", "Will we get paid?"

**NARRATOR:** "So why does this matter for front office staff?

Every patient interaction involves these three questions:

\[First thought bubble highlights\] Is this patient covered? That's eligibility verification—confirming their insurance is active and our clinic is in their network.

\[Second thought bubble highlights\]  
 What do they owe? That's understanding their benefits—copays, deductibles, coinsurance. We'll cover all of these terms in an upcoming lesson.

\[Third thought bubble highlights\] Will we get paid? That's making sure we have accurate insurance information so claims don't get denied.

When you understand how insurance works as a SYSTEM, these tasks make sense. You're not just collecting copays—you're making sure the financial relationship between patient, payer, and provider stays on track."

---

### **\[2:40-3:00\] KEY TAKEAWAY \+ TRANSITION**

**VISUAL:** Simple diagram showing: Premiums → Insurance Pool → Pays for Care

**NARRATOR:** "Here's what to remember:

Health insurance spreads the cost of care across many people. Patients pay premiums. Insurance companies manage the pool. Providers deliver care and bill insurance.

Your front desk role touches all three parts of this system—collecting patient information, verifying coverage with payers, and collecting what patients owe.

Next up: We'll look at the different TYPES of insurance—HMO, PPO, Medicare, Medicaid—and what each one means for how you do your job."

**VIDEO 2: Types of Payers & Plan Types**

**Duration:** 3 minutes **Learning Objective:** Distinguish between payer categories and understand plan type implications for front office workflows

## **SCRIPT**

### **\[0:00-0:20\] OPENING \- Why Plan Types Matter**

**VISUAL:** Title slide with question: "HMO vs. PPO—Why Should You Care?"

**NARRATOR:** "Now it’s time to learn about different types of insurance—HMO, PPO, EPO. Why does this matter at the front desk? Because the plan type determines whether you need to check for referrals, whether the patient can see any provider they want, and how much they'll pay out of pocket.

Let's break down the three main categories of insurance and the most common plan types you'll encounter."

---

### **\[0:20-1:20\] COMMERCIAL INSURANCE \- The Big Four Plan Types**

**VISUAL SLIDE 1:** Four-quadrant grid showing HMO, PPO, EPO, HDHP with icons

**NARRATOR:** "**Commercial insurance** is what most working adults have through their employers. Within commercial insurance, there are four main plan types.

**\[Quadrant 1 \- HMO highlights\]** **HMO—Health Maintenance Organization** Think of HMO as the most structured plan. Patients must choose a Primary Care Provider—or PCP—and they can only see that PCP unless they get a referral to a specialist. Everything happens inside the network. If you see 'HMO' on a card, your first question should be: 'Do you have a PCP assigned with us?' If not, you'll need to set that up before they can be seen.

**\[Quadrant 2 \- PPO highlights\]** **PPO—Preferred Provider Organization** PPO is the flexible option. Patients can see any provider they want—in-network or out-of-network—without a referral. They pay less if they stay in-network, but they have the freedom to go out-of-network if needed. No PCP required. No referral needed. This is the easiest plan type for front desk staff.

**\[Quadrant 3 \- EPO highlights\]** **EPO—Exclusive Provider Organization** EPO is the middle ground. Patients must stay in-network like an HMO, but they don't need a PCP or referrals. If they go out-of-network, the plan won't cover anything except emergencies. At check-in, make sure your clinic is in-network for their EPO plan.

**\[Quadrant 4 \- HDHP highlights\]** **HDHP—High Deductible Health Plan** HDHP isn't about network rules—it's about cost structure. These plans have very high deductibles—often $3,000 to $7,000—before insurance starts paying. Patients with HDHPs often have Health Savings Accounts, or HSAs, to help cover costs. At check-in, patients with HDHPs may owe more than a simple copay because they're still working toward their deductible."

---

### **\[1:20-2:20\] GOVERNMENT PROGRAMS**

**VISUAL SLIDE 2:** Three sections showing Medicare, Medicaid, and Military programs with icons

**NARRATOR:** "The second major category is **government programs**—insurance provided by federal or state governments.

**\[Medicare section highlights\]** **Medicare** Medicare is federal health insurance for people 65 and older, or those with certain disabilities. It has four parts:

* **Part A** covers hospital stays  
* **Part B** covers doctor visits and outpatient care  
* **Part C**—also called Medicare Advantage—is a private plan that replaces Parts A and B  
* **Part D** covers prescriptions

At the front desk, you'll mostly deal with Part B for office visits. Many Medicare patients also have supplemental insurance—called Medigap—that covers what Medicare doesn't.

**\[Medicaid section highlights\]** **Medicaid** Medicaid is state-run insurance for low-income individuals and families. Every state runs Medicaid differently, and some states use different names—like Medi-Cal in California. Eligibility rules vary, and benefits vary. Always verify Medicaid eligibility because coverage can change month to month based on income.

**\[Military section highlights\]** **Tricare and VA Benefits** These are insurance programs for active-duty military, veterans, and their families. Tricare works like commercial insurance with referral requirements depending on the plan. VA benefits are handled through VA facilities, so you'll rarely see VA patients at private clinics unless they have dual coverage."

---

### **\[2:20-2:50\] OTHER COVERAGE TYPES**

**VISUAL SLIDE 3:** Two additional boxes showing Workers' Comp and Auto Insurance

**NARRATOR:** "There are two more coverage types you need to know about, even though they're not traditional health insurance.

**\[Workers' Comp box highlights\]** **Workers' Compensation** If a patient was injured at work, their employer's workers' comp insurance pays for treatment related to that injury. These visits are billed differently than regular health insurance. Ask: 'Is this visit related to your work injury?' If yes, get the workers' comp claim number.

**\[Auto Insurance box highlights\]** **Auto Insurance / Motor Vehicle Accidents** If a patient was injured in a car accident, their auto insurance may be the primary payer—not their health insurance. Always ask if the visit is related to an accident. You'll need the auto insurance policy information and the claim number."

---

### **\[2:50-3:00\] KEY TAKEAWAY**

**VISUAL SLIDE 4:** Decision tree graphic showing: "See HMO? → Check for PCP. See PPO? → No referral needed. See Medicaid? → Verify monthly."

**NARRATOR:** "Here's your quick decision guide:

* See **HMO** on the card? Check if they have a PCP assigned.  
* See **PPO**? You're good to go—no referral needed.  
* See **Medicaid**? Verify eligibility every visit because coverage can change.

Understanding plan types means you'll catch potential issues before the patient gets to the exam room. That's the difference between smooth check-ins and frustrated patients.

Next up: How patients actually pay for care—copays, deductibles, and coinsurance."

# **LESSON 3: Key Insurance Terms**

**Duration:** 4 minutes  
 **Learning Objective:** Define essential insurance vocabulary and understand how each term affects patient financial responsibility.

---

## **SCRIPT**

### **\[0:00-0:20\] OPENING \- The Language of Insurance**

**VISUAL:** Title slide with scattered terms: "Premium," "Deductible," "Copay," "Coinsurance," "Out-of-Pocket Max"

**NARRATOR:** "You've learned why insurance exists and the different types of plans. Now let's decode the vocabulary.

These terms aren't just jargon—they determine how much patients pay and when. Understanding them means you can answer patient questions confidently and collect the right amount at check-in.

Let's start with what patients pay BEFORE they ever walk through your door."

---

### **\[0:20-0:50\] PREMIUM \- The Monthly Fee**

**VISUAL:** Calendar showing monthly payments ($400/month) being crossed off

**NARRATOR:** "A PREMIUM is the monthly fee patients pay just to HAVE insurance—whether they use it or not.

\[Calendar animation shows payments\] Think of it like a membership fee. You pay every month to belong to the insurance plan. If you stop paying, you lose coverage.

Premiums vary widely—some patients pay two hundred dollars a month, others pay eight hundred or more. The premium amount depends on the plan, the employer, and how many family members are covered.

Here's the key: Premiums are paid to the insurance company, not to your clinic. You won't collect premiums at the front desk. But if a patient's premium wasn't paid, their coverage may be terminated—which is why eligibility verification matters."

---

### **\[0:50-1:30\] DEDUCTIBLE \- The Annual Threshold**

**VISUAL:** Thermometer-style graphic showing $2,000 deductible, partially filled ($800 met, $1,200 remaining)

**NARRATOR:** "A DEDUCTIBLE is the amount a patient must pay out of their own pocket BEFORE insurance starts helping.

\[Thermometer shows $2,000 total\] Let's say a patient has a two-thousand-dollar deductible. That means they pay the first two thousand dollars of their medical bills each year—full price—before insurance kicks in.

\[Thermometer shows partial fill \- $800\] If they've already spent eight hundred dollars this year, they have twelve hundred dollars remaining on their deductible.

\[Thermometer fills more\] Once they hit that two thousand, insurance starts sharing the cost.

At check-in, this matters. If a patient hasn't met their deductible, they may owe much more than a simple copay. When you run eligibility verification, you can often see how much of their deductible has been met."

---

### **\[1:30-2:10\] COPAY \- The Fixed Amount**

**VISUAL:** Receipt showing "Office Visit \- $30 Copay" with a $30 bill being handed over

**NARRATOR:** "A COPAY is a fixed dollar amount the patient pays at the time of service.

\[Receipt displays\] Thirty dollars for a primary care visit. Fifty dollars for a specialist. These amounts are set by the insurance plan—they don't change based on what happens during the visit.

\[Money changing hands\] Copays are what you'll collect most often at check-in. The amount is usually printed right on the insurance card.

Important note: Some services don't have copays—instead, they're subject to deductible and coinsurance. And patients with High Deductible Health Plans may not have copays at all until they meet their deductible. Always verify."

---

### **\[2:10-2:50\] COINSURANCE \- The Percentage Split**

**VISUAL:** Pie chart showing 80/20 split, then a $500 bill splitting into $400 (insurance) and $100 (patient)

**NARRATOR:** "COINSURANCE is a percentage split between the patient and insurance—AFTER the deductible is met.

\[Pie chart shows 80/20\] A common split is 80/20. Insurance pays eighty percent, the patient pays twenty percent.

\[Bill splitting animation\] So if a service costs five hundred dollars and the patient has met their deductible, insurance pays four hundred and the patient owes one hundred.

Unlike a copay, coinsurance isn't a fixed amount—it's a percentage of the bill. The more expensive the service, the more the patient owes.

You won't always know the coinsurance amount at check-in because it depends on what services are provided. But understanding how it works helps you explain patient bills."

---

### **\[2:50-3:20\] OUT-OF-POCKET MAXIMUM \- The Safety Net**

**VISUAL:** Ceiling graphic showing $6,000 cap, with costs piling up and hitting the ceiling

**NARRATOR:** "The OUT-OF-POCKET MAXIMUM is the most a patient will pay in a year—their financial ceiling.

\[Costs pile up toward ceiling\] Let's say a patient has a six-thousand-dollar out-of-pocket max. Once they've paid six thousand dollars in deductibles, copays, and coinsurance combined, insurance covers one hundred percent of everything else for the rest of the year.

\[Ceiling hit, costs stop\] This protects patients from catastrophic expenses. If someone has major surgery or ongoing treatment, they won't pay more than their out-of-pocket max.

At check-in, if eligibility shows a patient has met their out-of-pocket maximum, they may owe nothing for the visit."

---

### **\[3:20-3:45\] IN-NETWORK vs. OUT-OF-NETWORK**

**VISUAL:** Map with clinic icons—some circled in green (in-network), others in red (out-of-network)

**NARRATOR:** "Two more terms you'll use daily: IN-NETWORK and OUT-OF-NETWORK.

\[Green circles highlight\] In-network means the provider has a contract with the insurance company. The patient gets better rates—lower copays, lower coinsurance.

\[Red circles highlight\] Out-of-network means no contract. The patient pays significantly more, and some plans—like HMOs and EPOs—won't cover out-of-network care at all.

At check-in, always confirm your clinic is in-network for the patient's plan. If not, let them know before they're seen so there are no surprise bills."

---

### **\[3:45-4:00\] KEY TAKEAWAY \+ TRANSITION**

**VISUAL:** Stacked summary showing the cost flow: Premium (monthly) → Deductible (annual threshold) → Copay/Coinsurance (per service) → Out-of-Pocket Max (annual ceiling)

**NARRATOR:** "Here's the payment flow:

Patients pay PREMIUMS monthly to have coverage. They pay their DEDUCTIBLE before insurance helps. Then they pay COPAYS or COINSURANCE for each service. And the OUT-OF-POCKET MAX caps their total annual cost.

Understanding these terms means you can answer patient questions, collect the right amounts, and explain why they owe what they owe.

You now have the foundation. In the next course, you'll put this knowledge to work—reading insurance cards, verifying eligibility, and collecting payments."

# **Module Summary**

With these three lessons complete, learners will have:

1. **Conceptual understanding** (Lesson 1): Why insurance exists, how risk pooling works, the patient-payer-provider triangle

2. **Payer knowledge** (Lesson 2 \- existing): Commercial plans (HMO, PPO, EPO, HDHP), government programs (Medicare, Medicaid, Tricare), other coverage (Workers' Comp, Auto)

3. **Vocabulary mastery** (Lesson 3): Premium, deductible, copay, coinsurance, out-of-pocket maximum, in-network/out-of-network

This prepares them for Course 2: Insurance Operations, where they'll APPLY this knowledge:

* Reading insurance cards (finding the terms they just learned)  
* Verifying eligibility (checking deductible status, network participation)  
* Collecting payments (knowing when to collect copays vs. other amounts)
