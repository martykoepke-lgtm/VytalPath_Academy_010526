# The "Do Not Use" List: When Shortcuts Can Kill

## Reading Lesson Content (Progressive Slides)

---

### A Single Letter

A doctor wrote "10U" on an insulin order.

She meant 10 units.

The nurse read it as "100."

The patient received ten times the intended dose. She went into a coma. She never woke up.

This isn't a hypothetical. This is why the Joint Commission created the "Do Not Use" list. These rules are written in the aftermath of preventable deaths.

**The good news?** You can prevent these errors. It starts with knowing which abbreviations are dangerous—and why.

---

### Why These Abbreviations Are Banned

The Joint Commission is the organization that accredits hospitals and healthcare facilities. In 2004, they said: enough.

They analyzed medication errors that hurt or killed patients. They found the same abbreviations showing up again and again.

So they created a mandatory list. These abbreviations must never be used on:
- Medication orders
- Prescriptions
- Preprinted forms
- Any medication-related documentation

If you see these abbreviations, that's a red flag. If you're ever asked to write them, don't.

---

### The "U" Problem

**The abbreviation:** U (for "unit")

**What goes wrong:** The letter "U" looks like a zero. It also looks like a 4. And sometimes like "cc."

**Real example:**
- Doctor writes: 10U insulin
- Nurse reads: 100 insulin
- Patient receives: 10x overdose

**The result:** Severe hypoglycemia, seizures, coma, death.

**Here's the thing:** About half of all prescription errors that result in death involve insulin. And this abbreviation is a major reason why.

**What to use instead:** Write "unit" or "units" — every single time.

---

### The "IU" Problem

**The abbreviation:** IU (for "International Unit")

**What goes wrong:** The "I" gets misread as a "1" or the letters blur together and look like "IV" (intravenous) or "10."

**Real example:**
- Order reads: 10 IU vitamin D
- Pharmacist sees: 10 IV
- Patient gets IV medication instead of oral

**Or worse:**
- Order reads: 5 IU
- Someone reads: 51U (fifty-one units)

**What to use instead:** Write "International Unit" — yes, the whole thing.

---

### The QD vs QOD Disaster

**The abbreviations:**
- QD (for "daily")
- QOD (for "every other day")

**What goes wrong:** They look almost identical. The "O" gets misread as "D." The period after "Q" gets misread as "I."

**Real example:**
- Doctor writes: QOD (every other day)
- Nurse reads: QID (four times daily)
- Patient receives: 8x the intended weekly dose

That's the difference between one dose every 48 hours and four doses every 24 hours. For some medications, that's fatal.

**What to use instead:** Write "daily" or "every other day" — in plain English.

---

### The Decimal Point Disasters

**Trailing zero problem:** 1.0 mg

The decimal point gets missed. Someone reads it as 10 mg. That's a 10x overdose.

**Rule:** Never write a zero after a decimal point. Write "1 mg" not "1.0 mg."

---

**Missing leading zero problem:** .5 mg

The decimal point gets missed. Someone reads it as 5 mg. That's also a 10x overdose.

**Rule:** Always write a zero before a decimal point. Write "0.5 mg" not ".5 mg."

---

**Here's why this matters:**

Many medications have a very narrow range between "therapeutic" and "toxic." A 10x error isn't just an inconvenience—it can stop a heart, destroy a liver, or cause permanent brain damage.

---

### The MS Confusion

**The abbreviations:**
- MS (could mean morphine sulfate OR magnesium sulfate)
- MSO4 (same problem)
- MgSO4 (same problem)

**What goes wrong:** These two drugs could not be more different:
- **Morphine sulfate** = powerful opioid painkiller
- **Magnesium sulfate** = treats seizures, low magnesium, preeclampsia

**Real example:**
- Nurse sees: MS 4mg IV
- Nurse thinks: Magnesium sulfate? Morphine sulfate?
- Guesses wrong
- Patient gets the wrong drug

**What to use instead:** Write the complete drug name. "Morphine sulfate" or "Magnesium sulfate" — every single time.

---

### The Extended Danger List

The Joint Commission's list is the mandatory minimum. But ISMP (Institute for Safe Medication Practices) maintains a longer list of abbreviations that have caused serious errors:

| Abbreviation | Intended | Misread As | Use Instead |
|--------------|----------|------------|-------------|
| µg | microgram | mg (1000x error!) | Write "mcg" |
| cc | cubic centimeter | U (units) | Write "mL" |
| HS or hs | at bedtime | "half-strength" | Write "at bedtime" or "nightly" |
| SC or SQ | subcutaneous | SL (sublingual) | Write "subcutaneous" |
| D/C | discharge or discontinue | Confused for each other | Write the full word |
| AD, AS, AU | right/left/both ears | OD, OS, OU (eyes) | Write "right ear," "left ear" |
| OD, OS, OU | right/left/both eyes | AD, AS, AU (ears) | Write "right eye," "left eye" |

### Your Role in Prevention

You might be thinking: "I'm front office. I don't write medication orders."

True. But you do:
- **Transcribe messages** from patients about their medications
- **Read faxes and referrals** that contain medication information
- **Enter data** into EHR systems
- **Communicate** between patients and clinical staff

If you see a dangerous abbreviation, you can:
1. **Flag it** for clarification before processing
2. **Ask** the clinical team what it means
3. **Never assume** — when in doubt, verify

The nurse who misread "10U" as "100" wasn't careless. She was busy, the handwriting was unclear, and she made a reasonable (but deadly) interpretation.

**The system failed her.** These rules exist so the system doesn't fail you—or your patients.

### Quick Reference: The Mandatory "Do Not Use" List

| DON'T Use | Problem | DO Use |
|-----------|---------|--------|
| U or u | Looks like 0, 4, or cc | "unit" |
| IU | Looks like IV or 10 | "International Unit" |
| QD, Q.D. | Confused with QID | "daily" |
| QOD, Q.O.D. | Confused with QD or QID | "every other day" |
| Trailing zero (1.0 mg) | Decimal missed → 10x dose | "1 mg" |
| No leading zero (.5 mg) | Decimal missed → 10x dose | "0.5 mg" |
| MS, MSO4, MgSO4 | Morphine vs Magnesium confusion | Write the full drug name |

---

### Key Takeaways

1. **The "Do Not Use" list exists because people died.** These aren't arbitrary rules—they're lessons learned from tragedy.

2. **The most dangerous errors are 10x overdoses** — caused by missed decimal points or "U" misread as "0."

3. **Insulin errors are the deadliest.** About half of fatal medication errors involve insulin.

4. **When in doubt, write it out.** "Unit" instead of "U." "Daily" instead of "QD." "Morphine sulfate" instead of "MS."

5. **Your role matters.** Even from the front office, you can catch these errors before they reach the patient.

*The best medical abbreviation is the one that can't be misread. When patient safety is on the line, clarity beats brevity every time.*
