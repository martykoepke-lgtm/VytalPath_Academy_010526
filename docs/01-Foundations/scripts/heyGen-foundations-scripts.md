# **VIDEO 1.0: Understanding How Healthcare is Delivered**

*Duration: 3 minutes*

### **SEGMENT 1: OPENING**

Welcome to Healthcare Foundations for Front Office Professionals\!

Before we dive into the specific skills you'll need—like scheduling, registration, and insurance verification—we need to start with something fundamental: understanding how healthcare is actually delivered.

Because here's the thing: the same administrative skills look very different depending on the care delivery model you're working in.

In this series, we're going to explore the two main ways healthcare is delivered, and why understanding this difference matters for your career.

### **SEGMENT 2: THE TWO MODELS**

Healthcare is delivered through two primary models.

First, there's Inpatient or Acute Care—this is hospital-based care. When a patient is admitted to a hospital, they receive continuous care within a single episode. Care comes to the patient at the bedside.

Second, there's Ambulatory or Outpatient Care—this is clinic-based care. Patients come to the clinic for appointments, then leave and go back to their daily lives. Care happens through multiple separate encounters over time.

These two models are fundamentally different.

In inpatient care, everything happens in one place, during one continuous episode. It's clinician-driven—doctors and nurses coordinate everything while the patient stays in bed.

In ambulatory care, patients navigate between multiple separate appointments across different locations and time periods. It's patient-driven—patients have to schedule, show up, and coordinate their own care between visits.

### **SEGMENT 3: WHY THIS MATTERS**

You might be thinking, "Why does this matter? Aren't the tasks the same?"

Well, yes and no. The core skills are the same—you'll do registration, scheduling, insurance verification, and coordination in both settings. But the context is completely different.

In a hospital, you're supporting a continuous episode happening right now, under one roof, with all the services controlled by one organization.

In an ambulatory clinic, you're coordinating across time, across multiple locations, and often across different organizations. You're bridging gaps between separate encounters that happen weeks or months apart.

Your work matters in both settings. But understanding the delivery model helps you understand WHY your work matters and HOW it fits into the bigger picture of patient care.

# **VIDEO 1.1: The Inpatient Encounter**

*Duration: 4–5 minutes*

### **SEGMENT 1: WHAT MAKES INPATIENT CARE UNIQUE**

The most important concept to understand is this: An inpatient encounter is a single, continuous episode of care.

From the moment a patient is admitted to the hospital until they're discharged, everything that happens is part of ONE encounter. All the care, all the services, all the documentation—it's all contained within this single episode.

If a patient is admitted on Monday with pneumonia and stays until Friday, every doctor visit, every medication, every lab test, every X-ray during those five days is part of that ONE inpatient encounter.

And here's the defining characteristic: Care comes to the patient. When someone is admitted, they become the stationary center of a coordinated care team. The doctor comes to examine them. Nurses monitor them around the clock. Lab technicians draw blood at the bedside. Medications are delivered and administered. The patient doesn't have to go anywhere—the care comes to them.

### **SEGMENT 2: EVERYTHING CONTAINED**

What makes inpatient care administratively unique is that everything is contained within one episode.

There's one medical record. Every doctor's note, every nursing assessment, every lab result, every medication given—it all goes into one comprehensive record that follows the patient throughout their stay.

There's one billing encounter. Most inpatient stays are billed under a bundled payment system—the hospital receives one payment that covers the entire admission. Compare this to outpatient care, where each visit and each test is billed separately.

And there's one care team. While individual nurses and doctors rotate shifts, there's continuity in the overall care plan. This is clinician-driven care—the medical team makes the decisions and coordinates everything in a controlled environment.

### **SEGMENT 3: ADMINISTRATIVE TOUCHPOINTS**

Even though inpatient care is clinician-driven, there are critical administrative functions that support the entire episode.

At admission—whether through the Emergency Department or a scheduled procedure—registration staff verify identity, capture insurance information, obtain consents, and secure necessary authorizations.

During the stay, health information staff coordinate medical records from other providers and ensure documentation is complete.

At discharge, the team coordinates home health services, medical equipment, and follow-up appointments—verifying insurance coverage for each.

After discharge, coding and billing staff review the record, assign diagnosis codes, and submit claims.

### **SEGMENT 4: TRANSITION**

Understanding how hospitals work gives you essential context—because in an outpatient clinic, you'll interact with patients who've just been discharged. You'll receive records from hospital stays. You'll help patients schedule follow-up care.

Now let's shift gears completely and explore ambulatory care—where patients must coordinate their own care between visits, and where the front office becomes the essential communication hub.

# **VIDEO 1.2: The Ambulatory Care Journey**

*Duration: 5 minutes*

### **SEGMENT 1: WHAT IS AMBULATORY CARE**

Ambulatory care is where most healthcare actually happens—in clinics, imaging centers, labs, and therapy offices. And the core concept is this: Ambulatory care happens through multiple discrete encounters across time.

Here's an example. A patient sees their primary care doctor for a checkup. The doctor orders lab work—that's a separate appointment scheduled for the following week. Results come back and lead to a specialist referral—but the first available appointment is two months out. The specialist orders an MRI—but the imaging center is booked six weeks out. And before the MRI can happen, insurance needs to approve it.

From the initial doctor visit to the MRI, months have passed across seven or eight separate encounters. Each encounter is discrete, with defined start and end points. And between each one, there are gaps—time periods of waiting, coordinating, and trying to move things forward.

### **SEGMENT 2: THE PATIENT MUST COORDINATE**

In ambulatory care, the patient must go to the care. They have to call to schedule appointments, drive to different locations, remember pre-appointment instructions, show up on time, and follow up when results don't come back.

The patient becomes the mobile coordinator of their own care. This is why we call it patient-driven care.

And here's the challenge: Not every patient is equipped to do this well. Some are highly organized and stay on top of everything. But many struggle—they forget to schedule, miss appointments, don't understand next steps, or get overwhelmed by insurance paperwork. This is where the front office becomes essential.

### **SEGMENT 3: WHAT HAPPENS BETWEEN VISITS**

Between visits is where all the complexity lives. There are timing challenges—imaging centers are often booked four to six weeks out, specialists may have wait times of two to three months.

There's insurance authorization. Most downstream services require separate approval. The clinic submits a request, provides documentation, waits for approval, and coordinates scheduling once it's granted. If it's denied, the process starts over with an appeal.

And the patient must follow through—scheduling, showing up, arranging transportation, bringing paperwork, following prep requirements. If they don't, the care plan stalls.

### **SEGMENT 4: THE FRONT OFFICE AS COMMUNICATION HUB**

The front office acts as a three-way communication hub.

You manage inbound communication—lab results, imaging reports, consult notes, patient calls, insurance updates. All of it has to be received, routed, and acted upon.

You manage outbound communication—referrals to specialists, authorization requests, records to other providers, patient reminders.

And you manage internal communication—provider updates, patient callbacks, tracking pending orders. You're the one making sure nothing falls through the cracks.

### **SEGMENT 5: CLOSING**

Your job isn't just to schedule appointments and check patients in. Your job is to bridge the gaps between encounters and ensure that the care plan actually gets completed.

You verify insurance before patients show up. You track authorizations. You remind patients about appointments. You coordinate between the clinic, lab, imaging center, and specialists. You communicate when results are back or next steps are needed.

Without you, patients would get lost in the system. Orders would go unfilled. Care plans would stall.

You are the essential link that holds a complex, fragmented system together.
