## **SCENE 2: The Default Rule (0:25-1:15)**

### **Avatar Script:**

\[emphatic\] Here's the fundamental rule. \[pause\] Patient health information belongs to the patient. \[pause\]

\[matter-of-fact\] Being a spouse, \[pause\] parent of an adult child, \[pause\] sibling, \[pause\] or best friend \[pause\] does not automatically grant access. \[pause\]

\[serious\] Without the patient's permission, you cannot share their information with anyone, \[emphasis\] no matter how close the relationship. \[pause\]

\[conversational\] Let me give you an example. \[pause\] A husband calls asking for his wife's lab results. \[pause\] He says she's at work and asked him to call. \[pause\]

\[questioning\] Can you give him the results? \[long pause\]

\[firm\] No. \[pause\] Not unless there's written authorization on file. \[pause\]

\[informative\] Even if he's listed as an emergency contact, \[pause\] that's not the same as authorization to receive medical information. \[pause\]

\[reassuring\] The patient must specifically authorize who can receive their health information. \[pause\] This is typically done through an authorization form \[pause\] that names the specific person, \[pause\] what information can be shared, \[pause\] and how long the authorization is valid. \[pause\]

\[serious\] Without this documentation, you cannot release information, \[emphasis\] no matter how sincere the caller sounds. \[pause\]

\[friendly\] Here's a situation that happens every day. \[pause\] A patient brings someone with them to an appointment. \[pause\] A spouse, \[pause\] an adult child, \[pause\] a friend. \[pause\]

\[professional\] Say something like, \[pause\] \[warm\] "Do we have authorization to discuss your spouse's information with you?" \[pause\]

\[reassuring\] This protects the patient \[pause\] and protects you. \[pause\] \[emphatic\] Never assume that just because someone is family, they're authorized to receive information. \[pause\]

## **SCENE 4: Release of Information (2:15-3:15)**

### **Avatar Script:**

\[informative\] When patients want their records sent somewhere, \[pause\] to a specialist, \[pause\] an attorney, \[pause\] an insurance company, \[pause\] or even to themselves, \[pause\] they complete a Release of Information form, \[pause\] commonly called an ROI. \[pause\]

\[serious\] This is a legal document with specific requirements. \[pause\]

\[instructional\] A valid ROI must include: \[pause\]

\[clear\] The patient's name and identifying information. \[pause\] Exactly what information is being released. \[pause\] Who it's being released to. \[pause\] The purpose of the release. \[pause\] An expiration date or event. \[pause\] And the patient's signature and date. \[pause\]

\[firm\] If any of these elements are missing, \[pause\] the ROI isn't valid, \[pause\] and you cannot release the records. \[pause\]

\[thoughtful\] Patients can revoke their authorization at any time \[pause\] by notifying your office in writing. \[pause\]

\[matter-of-fact\] Once revoked, \[pause\] you can no longer share information with that person or organization, \[pause\] even if the original authorization hasn't expired. \[pause\]

\[encouraging\] Always check that an authorization is still current before releasing any information. \[pause\]

## **SCENE 5: Document Security (3:15-4:15)**

### **Avatar Script:**

\[thoughtful\] Authorization isn't just about what you say. \[pause\] \[emphatic\] It's also about what people can see. \[pause\]

\[serious\] Papers with patient information can never be left in public view. \[pause\]

\[conversational\] That insurance card you just copied. \[pause\] The intake form in your to-do pile. \[pause\] The lab requisition waiting to be filed. \[pause\]

\[emphatic\] Every one of those is protected health information \[pause\] that unauthorized people could see. \[pause\]

\[instructional\] Keep documents face-down on your desk \[pause\] or inside folders. \[pause\]

\[firm\] Never leave patient paperwork visible when you step away, \[pause\] \[emphasis\] even for a moment. \[pause\]

\[matter-of-fact\] Other patients walking by. \[pause\] Delivery drivers. \[pause\] Visitors. \[pause\] Anyone could see information they shouldn't. \[pause\]

\[friendly\] The same goes for sticky notes. \[pause\] If you jot down a patient's name and callback number, \[pause\] that scrap of paper is now PHI. \[pause\]

\[instructional\] Position computer screens so patients can't see them. \[pause\] Use privacy filters if needed. \[pause\]

\[emphatic\] Lock your workstation every time you step away, \[pause\] \[emphasis\] even for 30 seconds. \[pause\]

\[matter-of-fact\] And at the end of each day, \[pause\] your desk should be clear of any patient information. \[pause\] File it. \[pause\] Lock it up. \[pause\] Or shred it. \[pause\]

\[encouraging\] A clean desk isn't just organized. \[pause\] \[warm\] It's HIPAA compliant. \[pause\]

## **SCENE 6: Handling Common Requests (4:15-4:50)**

### **Avatar Script:**

\[helpful\] When someone asks you for patient information, \[pause\] train yourself to pause and ask three questions. \[pause\]

\[clear\] First, \[pause\] is this person the patient? \[pause\]

\[clear\] Second, \[pause\] if not, do we have written authorization on file? \[pause\]

\[clear\] Third, \[pause\] am I certain this authorization covers what they're asking for? \[pause\]

\[firm\] If the answer to any of these is no, \[pause\] or you're not sure, \[pause\] \[emphatic\] don't share. \[pause\]

\[reassuring\] It's always okay to say: \[pause\]

\[professional\] "I need to verify that we have authorization on file. Can you hold for just a moment?" \[pause\]

\[helpful\] Or: \[pause\] \[professional\] "I'm not able to share that information without the patient's written permission. I can provide you with an authorization form." \[pause\]

\[warm\] Being careful isn't being rude. \[pause\] \[emphatic\] It's protecting the patient. \[pause\]

# **HeyGen Tag Reference**

*Use these tags in your HeyGen script to control avatar delivery:*

## **Pause Tags**

**\[pause\]** — Brief pause (0.5-1 second)

**\[long pause\]** — Extended pause (1.5-2 seconds)

## **Emotion Tags**

**\[friendly\]** — Warm, welcoming tone

**\[warm\]** — Caring, personable delivery

**\[serious\]** — Grave, important tone

**\[firm\]** — Definitive, clear stance

**\[encouraging\]** — Supportive, positive

**\[reassuring\]** — Calming, confidence-building

**\[thoughtful\]** — Reflective, considered

**\[conversational\]** — Natural, casual delivery

**\[professional\]** — Formal, business-like

**\[instructional\]** — Teaching mode

**\[matter-of-fact\]** — Neutral, factual

**\[curious\]** — Questioning, engaging

**\[questioning\]** — Rhetorical question delivery

**\[understanding\]** — Empathetic acknowledgment

**\[helpful\]** — Eager to assist

**\[sincere\]** — Genuine, heartfelt

**\[informative\]** — Educational, knowledge-sharing

**\[clear\]** — Articulate, easy to follow

## **Emphasis Tags**

**\[emphatic\]** — Strong emphasis on following phrase

**\[emphasis\]** — Stress the immediately following word(s)

## **Usage Tips**

1\. Place emotion tags at the start of a sentence or clause to set the tone.

2\. Use \[pause\] after key points to let information sink in.

3\. Use \[long pause\] before revealing answers or after questions.

4\. Combine tags: \[emphatic\] sets overall tone, \[emphasis\] stresses specific words.

5\. Don't over-tag. One emotion tag per sentence is usually sufficient.
